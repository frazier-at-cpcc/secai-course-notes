### Human-centric AI design principles
- ==Human-centric AI design principles==: build the system around the people who use it and the people it affects
  - The AI supports a decision; a person owns the decision and its consequences
- Principles that become security requirements

| Principle | In a SOC assistant |
|---|---|
| Human agency | analyst can accept, edit, or reject every suggestion |
| Transparency | users know they are dealing with AI |
| Explainability | each verdict shows its evidence and sources |
| Accountability | a named owner for the system and each action |
| Contestability | a wrong decision can be appealed and reversed |
| Calibrated trust | confidence shown; uncertainty not hidden |
| Privacy and safety | least data, least privilege, safe failure |

- The main human failure is ==automation bias==: trusting machine output because it came from a machine
  - Worse when the AI is usually right, the queue is long, and rejecting takes more clicks than approving
  - Design against it: show evidence, make rejection one click, sample-check accepted answers
- These ideas recur in the OECD AI Principles, the NIST AI RMF trustworthiness characteristics, and the EU AI Act (objectives 4.2 and 4.3)

### How much human involvement
- Match the level of involvement to the impact and reversibility of the action, not to the model's confidence alone

:::wide
| Level | Who decides | SOC example | Fits when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human-in-the-loop | a person approves each action before it runs | isolate a host, disable an account | high impact or hard to reverse |
| ==Human-on-the-loop== | the system acts; a person watches and can stop it | auto-close duplicate phishing reports | high volume, low impact, reversible |
| ==Human-out-of-the-loop== | fully automated | add an IOC to a watchlist, enrich an alert | trivial impact, easily undone |
:::

- A level is chosen per action, not per system
  - The same assistant may enrich alerts on its own and still need approval to block a domain
- Unchecked autonomy is an OWASP risk: `LLM06:2025 Excessive Agency`
  - Mitigations include limiting tools and permissions and requiring human approval for high-impact actions

### Human-in-the-loop
- ==Human-in-the-loop== (HITL): the AI proposes, a person approves, then the action runs
- Design rules for an approval gate
  - Gate by action type and impact; a 0.91-confidence account disable still waits for a person
  - ==Fail closed==: no answer, a timeout, or an error means no action
  - A named approver, never a shared account; record who, what, when, and why
  - Show the approver the evidence behind the verdict
  - Separate duties: the person who wrote a rule should not approve its first actions

:::figure 1.3-approval-gate.svg | An approval gate. Only low-impact, high-confidence actions skip the human; every path writes to the audit log.

```console label="Terminal"
$ export APPROVER=jlee
$ printf 'y\nn\n' | python3 gate.py proposals.json
P-1 watchlist 198.51.100.7  conf 0.97
  auto by policy
P-2 isolate_host ws-0142  conf 0.62
  approve? [y/N] y
  approved by jlee
P-3 disable_account svc-backup  conf 0.91
  approve? [y/N] n
  denied by jlee
P-4 block_domain cdn.example.net  conf 0.88
  approve? [y/N] 
  denied by default
```

- Reading the run
  - `P-1` is a reversible watchlist entry at high confidence, so policy lets it through
  - `P-3` was high confidence, but disabling a service account can break backups; the analyst said no
  - `P-4` got no answer and was denied by default

```console label="Terminal"
$ jq -c '[.id, .decision, .by]' audit.jsonl
["P-1","auto","policy"]
["P-2","approved","jlee"]
["P-3","denied","jlee"]
["P-4","denied","default"]
```

- Watch for ==approval fatigue==
  - If analysts approve 99% of requests in two seconds each, the gate is a rubber stamp
  - Track approval rates and decision times; move truly safe actions out of the gate so real ones get attention

### Human oversight
- ==Human oversight==: people supervise the AI system as a whole and can intervene
  - Broader than one approval: it covers the system's behavior over time
- What oversight needs
  - Visibility: dashboards of actions taken, error rates, overrides, and drift
  - Authority and means to act: override an output, pause automation, roll back a model, a ==kill switch==
  - Trained people who understand what the system can and cannot do
  - Regular review of a sample of automated decisions, not only the escalated ones
- The EU AI Act (Article 14) requires high-risk AI systems to be designed for effective human oversight
  - Includes the ability to disregard or override output and to stop the system, and awareness of automation bias
- Oversight is also a security control
  - A person who sees the assistant suddenly closing every alert from one subnet can catch a poisoned model or a manipulated prompt

### Human validation
- ==Human validation==: people check that data and outputs are actually correct
- Where it happens in the life cycle
  - Labels: reviewers check a sample of labeled training data; disagreements are resolved before training
  - Evaluation: human graders and red teamers judge answers that metrics cannot score
  - Production: analysts confirm or correct a sample of verdicts; their corrections become ground truth
  - Feedback: reviewed before it re-enters training, so an attacker cannot steer the next model
- Techniques
  - Gold questions with known answers mixed into labeling work, to measure reviewer accuracy
  - Two reviewers on high-impact items, measuring how often they agree (==inter-annotator agreement==)
  - Sampling rates that rise with risk: every output for legal notices, 2% for alert enrichment
- How the three terms differ
  - Human-in-the-loop gates individual actions; human oversight supervises the whole system; human validation checks correctness of data and output

:::exam
- A scenario where the AI must not act until a person approves is human-in-the-loop. A person monitoring and able to stop an autonomous system is human oversight (human-on-the-loop). Reviewing samples of output or labels for correctness is human validation.
- High-impact or irreversible actions (isolating hosts, disabling accounts, sending external messages) need approval regardless of the model's confidence score. Missing approval should fail closed.
- Automation bias and approval fatigue are the human weaknesses that make oversight ineffective; the fix is evidence-rich, easy-to-reject review and monitoring of approval rates.
:::
