### Introduction of bias
- ==Introduction of bias==: the model learns or amplifies unfair patterns and produces skewed outcomes for some groups
- Where bias gets in

| Source | Example |
|---|---|
| Historical data | Past hiring favored one group; the model copies it |
| Sampling | Phishing classifier trained only on English mail misses other languages |
| Labeling | Analysts label one team's alerts "noise" more often |
| Proxy features | ZIP code standing in for ethnicity |
| Feedback loop | The model's own decisions become next month's training data |

- Real case: Amazon dropped an experimental résumé-screening model after finding it downgraded résumés that mentioned "women's" (reported by Reuters, 2018)
  - Trained on ten years of mostly male hiring history
- Business impact: discrimination claims, regulatory action, bad decisions at scale
- Controls: representative data, bias metrics per group before release and in production, human review of adverse decisions, an ==impact assessment== for systems that judge people

### Accidental data leakage
- ==Accidental data leakage==: sensitive data leaves its intended boundary through an AI system, with no attacker required
- How it happens
  - Users paste source code, customer records, or incident details into a public chatbot whose terms allow the provider to retain or train on prompts
  - A RAG assistant indexes a SharePoint site with broken permissions and answers anyone's question with HR files
  - A model memorizes training records and repeats them on request
  - Prompts and responses land in logs, analytics, or a vendor's abuse-monitoring store with long retention
- Real case: in 2023 Samsung engineers pasted internal source code into ChatGPT; Samsung then restricted generative AI tools on company devices
- Maps to ==LLM02:2025 Sensitive Information Disclosure== in the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications
- Controls: approved tools with no-training and retention terms, DLP and redaction at the AI gateway, permission-aware retrieval, log minimization, awareness training

### Accuracy, performance, and reputational loss
- ==Accuracy and performance of the model==: a model can be confidently wrong, and it can get worse over time
  - Hallucination (NIST calls it ==confabulation==): fluent, plausible, false output
  - ==Model drift==: the world changes (new attack techniques, new products) and accuracy decays with no code change
  - Latency and cost: a model too slow or expensive for its workload fails in a different way
- Real cases
  - Mata v. Avianca (US District Court, 2023): lawyers fined after filing a brief citing court cases ChatGPT invented
  - Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024): a tribunal held the airline liable for refund terms its website chatbot made up; "the chatbot is responsible" was rejected
- ==Reputational loss==: public harm to trust and brand when AI behaves badly in view of customers
  - Microsoft's Tay chatbot (2016) was pulled within a day after users taught it to post offensive content
  - Screenshots travel faster than corrections; one bad answer becomes the story
- Controls: evaluation sets and acceptance thresholds, grounding with cited sources, human review for external output, drift monitoring, a rollback plan, a communications plan for AI incidents

### Intellectual Property (IP)-related risks
- ==Intellectual Property (IP)-related risks== run in both directions
- Inbound: the model or its output infringes someone else's rights
  - Training data scraped without license; copyright suits such as The New York Times v. OpenAI and Microsoft (filed 2023)
  - Generated code that reproduces licensed code (GPL) without attribution
  - Open-weight model licenses that restrict commercial use or require attribution
- Outbound: the organization loses its own IP
  - Trade secrets or unreleased designs pasted into a public model
  - Output may not be protectable: US law requires human authorship for copyright (Thaler v. Perlmutter, D.C. Circuit, 2025)
- Controls: legal review of model and data licenses, vendor ==IP indemnification== clauses, code scanning for license matches, rules on what may enter prompts

### Autonomous systems
- ==Autonomous systems==: AI that takes actions, not just answers: agents that call tools, run scripts, send email, change configurations, or move money
- Risks grow with autonomy
  - A wrong answer becomes a wrong action, often at machine speed and before anyone notices
  - Prompt injection in a document or web page can steer the agent's tool calls
  - Unclear liability when an agent acts on its own
- OWASP calls this ==LLM06:2025 Excessive Agency==: too much functionality, permission, or autonomy
- Controls
  - Least-privilege tool scopes and short-lived credentials
  - Human approval for high-impact or irreversible actions
  - Rate and spend limits, full action logging, and a kill switch
  - Sandboxed execution for any code the agent writes

### Shadow IT and shadow AI
- ==Shadow IT==: technology used without IT's knowledge or approval (a personal Dropbox, an unapproved SaaS app)
- ==Shadow AI==: the same pattern for AI: unapproved chatbots, browser extensions, meeting transcription bots, AI features switched on inside approved SaaS, or a team's self-hosted model nobody registered

:::figure wide 4.2-shadow-ai.svg | The sanctioned path redacts, logs, and routes through a gateway. Shadow AI skips every control, so nobody can see or recall what was sent.

- Why shadow AI is worse than ordinary shadow IT
  - Every prompt is a potential data transfer to a third party
  - Output feeds decisions with no review, no accuracy check, and no record
  - Many tools are free and need only a browser, so procurement never sees them
- Finding it
  - Secure web gateway, CASB, and DNS logs for traffic to known AI domains
  - Browser extension inventory from endpoint management; OAuth grants in the identity provider
  - Expense reports and corporate card statements for AI subscriptions
  - Surveys and amnesty: ask teams what they use before blocking it
- Fixing it: give people a sanctioned tool that is good enough, then block or coach on the rest (objective 4.3)

:::exam
- Customer data pasted into a free public chatbot is accidental data leakage through shadow AI. Fix it with an approved alternative plus policy and DLP; a block alone pushes use to personal phones.
- Accuracy falling over months with no code change is model drift.
- An agent that deletes records without human approval is an autonomous-system risk (excessive agency).
:::
