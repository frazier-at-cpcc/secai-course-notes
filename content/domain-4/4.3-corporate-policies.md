### Corporate policies
- ==Corporate policies== for AI translate laws, contracts, and risk appetite into rules for employees, developers, and vendors
  - Regulations set the floor; customer contracts and the company's own risk appetite usually go further
- The usual set
  - AI acceptable use policy: approved tools, forbidden data, human review of output, disclosure of AI-generated work
  - AI development policy: testing, documentation, and approval gates before release
  - Data classification and handling, extended to prompts, embeddings, training sets, and logs
  - Third-party and procurement policy: what a vendor must prove before purchase
- Compliance shapes how the business uses AI
  - Some uses are off the table entirely (prohibited practices, regulated decisions without human review)
  - Others need extra time and evidence: impact assessments, conformity documentation, customer notices
- A policy needs enforcement to matter
  - SSO-only access to approved AI tools; an ==AI gateway== in front of model APIs; DLP on prompts; logging
  - A documented ==exception process== with an expiry date, so teams ask instead of working around the rule

### Sanctioned vs. unsanctioned
- ==Sanctioned vs. unsanctioned==: whether the organization has reviewed and approved an AI tool for a given use and data class
  - Sanctioned: contract signed, security and privacy review done, SSO and logging enabled, listed in the AI inventory
  - Unsanctioned: anything else, including a personal account on an approved product
- Most organizations publish three lists

| Status | Meaning | Example handling |
|---|---|---|
| Approved | Use for listed data classes | Enterprise tenant via SSO |
| Tolerated | Public data only; under review | Allowed, coached by a browser banner |
| Prohibited | Must not be used | Blocked at the web gateway |

- Approval is per use and per data class
  - A tool sanctioned for marketing copy is not automatically sanctioned for customer records
- Blocking alone fails; people move to personal phones
  - Pair every block with a sanctioned alternative and a fast way to request new tools

### Private vs. public models
- ==Public models==: hosted services open to anyone, usually consumer or free tiers
  - Prompts may be retained, reviewed by the provider, or used for training under consumer terms
  - No company contract, no SSO, no audit logs available to the company
- ==Private models==: models the organization controls or has contracted for exclusive, isolated use
  - Enterprise tenant of a hosted model: contractual no-training, set retention, regional processing, admin logs
  - Self-hosted open-weight model in the company's own cloud account or data center: data never leaves; the company carries patching, scaling, and model security
- The terms also describe model weights: open-weight models anyone can download versus proprietary models reachable only through an API

:::wide
| | Public service | Enterprise tenant (hosted) | Self-hosted private model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where prompts go | Provider, under consumer terms | Provider, under contract | Your infrastructure |
| Training on your data | Possible; check terms | Contractually excluded | Only if you choose |
| Logging and audit | None for you | Admin and audit logs | Full control |
| Effort and cost | Lowest | Subscription | Hardware, staff, patching |
| Typical data allowed | Public only | Internal and confidential | Up to restricted |
:::

### Sensitive data governance
- ==Sensitive data governance== decides which data may reach which AI system, in what form, and for how long
- Classify first: public, internal, confidential, restricted (PII, PHI, cardholder data, credentials, source code, M&A plans)
  - Map each class to the model tiers allowed to process it

:::figure wide 4.3-data-routing.svg | Classification drives routing. The gateway inspects each prompt, redacts what policy allows it to redact, and sends the request only to a model tier approved for that data class.

- Controls across the AI data flow
  - Prompts: DLP and redaction at the gateway; block secrets and card numbers outright
  - Training and fine-tuning data: documented lawful basis, minimization, de-identification; a record of what went into each model
  - RAG sources: index only what the requesting user may already read; filter retrieval by the user's permissions
  - Outputs and logs: classify them like the inputs that produced them; set retention; restrict who can read prompt logs
- Regulated data brings its own rules: GDPR for EU personal data, HIPAA for US health data, PCI DSS for cardholder data

### Third-party compliance evaluations
- ==Third-party compliance evaluations==: due diligence on every AI vendor, model provider, and AI feature inside an existing SaaS product
  - The vendor's model is part of your attack surface and your compliance scope
- Evidence to request
  - Independent reports and certifications: ==SOC 2 Type II==, ISO/IEC 27001, ISO/IEC 42001, FedRAMP authorization for US federal work
  - Data processing agreement: no training on your data, retention period, deletion on termination, breach notice
  - Subprocessor list and processing locations, including where inference runs
  - Model documentation: model card, evaluation and red-team results, known limitations
  - Security testing: recent penetration test summary, vulnerability disclosure program
  - Regulatory position: the vendor's role under the EU AI Act (provider) and its conformity evidence for high-risk uses
- Contract terms worth fighting for: right to audit, notice before model or subprocessor changes, IP indemnification
- Reassess on a schedule and when the vendor changes its model, terms, or hosting region

### Data sovereignty
- ==Data sovereignty==: data is subject to the laws of the country where it is collected or stored, and that country's authorities can reach it
- Related terms
  - ==Data residency==: where the data physically sits (a business or contractual choice)
  - ==Data localization==: a legal requirement that certain data stay inside a country
- Why AI makes it harder
  - Inference can run in a different region from storage; a prompt sent to an EU-stored tenant may be processed elsewhere unless regional processing is set
  - Training data, embeddings, logs, and backups each have a location
  - A provider headquartered in one country may be compelled by its home law (the US CLOUD Act) to produce data stored abroad
- Transfer rules
  - GDPR Chapter V restricts transfers of personal data out of the EU: adequacy decisions (such as the EU-US Data Privacy Framework), standard contractual clauses, binding corporate rules
  - Other countries have their own regimes, such as China's PIPL
- Controls: pin storage and inference to approved regions, customer-managed encryption keys, sovereign cloud offerings, self-hosted models for the most restricted data

:::exam
- "Employees use a personal account on an approved AI product" is still unsanctioned use; sanctioning covers the tenant, contract, and data class, not just the brand.
- Data residency is where data is stored; data sovereignty is whose laws govern it. A question about a foreign government compelling access is a sovereignty question.
- Before buying an AI service, a third-party compliance evaluation asks for SOC 2 Type II or ISO certifications, a data processing agreement with no-training terms, and processing locations.
:::
