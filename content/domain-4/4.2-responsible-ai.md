### Principles that turn into requirements
- ==Responsible AI== is designing, building, and using AI so that it is fair, safe, private, understandable, and someone answers for it
  - A principle is only useful once it becomes a test, a control, or a sign-off
- Most published principle sets cover the same ground
  - Microsoft's Responsible AI Standard: fairness, reliability and safety, privacy and security, inclusiveness, transparency, accountability
  - NIST AI RMF "trustworthy" characteristics and the OECD AI Principles say much the same in other words (objective 4.3)
- The exam list adds explainability, consistency, and awareness training

:::wide
| Principle | The question it asks | Evidence it produces |
|---|---|---|
| Fairness | Does it treat comparable people alike? | Bias metrics per group, fairness review |
| Reliability and safety | Does it behave as intended, and fail safely? | Evaluation results, red-team report, fallback design |
| Consistency | Same input, same answer, release after release? | Pinned versions, regression test suite |
| Transparency | Do people know AI is involved and what it is for? | User disclosure, model card, data sheet |
| Explainability | Can we say why it produced this output? | Feature attributions, cited sources, reason codes |
| Privacy and security | Is personal data protected and the system defended? | DPIA, access controls, privacy techniques |
| Inclusiveness | Does it work for everyone who must use it? | Accessibility and language testing, diverse testers |
| Accountability | Who owns the outcome and can stop it? | Named owner, approvals, audit trail |
| Awareness training | Do people know its limits? | Training records, AI literacy program |
:::

### Fairness and inclusiveness
- ==Fairness==: outcomes do not differ across groups (age, sex, ethnicity, disability) for reasons unrelated to the task
  - A model can be unfair without ever seeing a protected attribute: ZIP code, school, or name can act as a proxy
- Measure it; do not assume it
  - ==Demographic parity==: each group receives the positive outcome at a similar rate
  - Equalized odds: error rates (false positives and negatives) are similar across groups
  - The four-fifths rule from US employment guidance: a selection rate below 80% of the highest group's rate is a red flag
  - The metrics can conflict; the governance committee picks which one fits the use case
- ==Inclusiveness==: the system works for the full range of people who will use it or be judged by it
  - Speech and vision models trained mostly on one accent or skin tone fail others
  - Accessible interfaces, plain-language output, multilingual testing
  - Include affected groups when defining requirements and testing

### Reliability, safety, and consistency
- ==Reliability and safety==: the system performs as intended under expected and unexpected conditions, and fails in a way that does not cause harm
  - Test with edge cases, adversarial prompts, and out-of-distribution inputs before release
  - Safe failure: a SOC triage assistant that is unsure escalates to a human instead of closing the alert
  - Guardrails, rate limits, and a kill switch for agents
- ==Consistency==: similar inputs produce similar outputs, today and after the next update
  - LLMs are probabilistic; set temperature low, pin the model version, version the system prompt
  - A provider silently updating a hosted model changes behavior with no change on your side
  - Keep a regression suite of known prompts and expected answers; run it on every model or prompt change
  - Consistency also means applying the same decision logic to every case and group

### Transparency and explainability
- ==Transparency==: openness about the fact that AI is used, what it does, what data it was trained on, and its known limits
  - Tell users when they are talking to a chatbot or seeing generated content
  - Publish a model card: intended use, training data summary, evaluation results, limitations
- ==Explainability==: the ability to say why the model produced a particular output
  - Interpretable by design: a decision tree or a linear scorecard
  - Post hoc for complex models: SHAP and LIME feature attributions, counterfactuals ("approved if income were 5% higher")
  - For RAG assistants: cite the retrieved source passages so a human can verify the answer
  - An LLM's written reasoning is not proof of how it reached the answer
- Regulated decisions (credit, hiring, insurance) often require a reason the affected person can understand

### Privacy, security, and differential privacy
- ==Privacy and security==: personal data is collected lawfully, used only for its purpose, and protected; the AI system resists attack
  - Data minimization, consent, retention limits, and access control apply to training data, prompts, and logs
  - Models can memorize training records; membership inference and extraction attacks recover them (objective 2.6)
- ==Differential privacy== (DP) adds calibrated random noise so the output barely changes whether any one person's record is included or not
  - ==Epsilon== (ε), the privacy budget: smaller epsilon means more noise and stronger privacy
  - Formal promise: adding or removing one person changes the probability of any result by at most a factor of e<sup>ε</sup>
  - Laplace mechanism for counts: add noise with scale (sensitivity ÷ ε); a count's sensitivity is 1
  - Every query spends budget; repeated queries add their epsilons together
- Where DP is used
  - Releasing statistics: the US Census Bureau protected 2020 census tables with DP
  - Training: DP-SGD clips each example's gradient and adds noise, limiting what the model can memorize

:::figure 4.2-dp-laplace.svg | The Laplace mechanism. Lower epsilon widens the noise, so the result says less about any one person.

- Demonstration: release "how many users pasted secrets into the chatbot this week" (true count 42) at two epsilon values

```python label="dp_count.py"
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
TRUE = 42      # users who pasted secrets
SENS = 1       # one person changes it by 1

def noisy(count, eps, n):
    scale = SENS / eps
    return count + rng.laplace(0, scale, n)

for eps in (1.0, 0.1):
    one = noisy(TRUE, eps, 1)[0]
    d = noisy(TRUE, eps, 200_000)
    d2 = noisy(TRUE + 1, eps, 200_000)
    err = np.mean(np.abs(d - TRUE))
    p, p2 = np.mean(d > 42.5), np.mean(d2 > 42.5)
    print(f"eps={eps}: answer={one:6.1f}"
          f"  avg_err={err:5.2f}"
          f"  ratio={p2 / p:4.2f}")
print(f"e^1={np.e:.2f}  e^0.1={np.exp(0.1):.2f}")
```

```console label="Terminal"
$ python3 dp_count.py
eps=1.0: answer=  41.0  avg_err= 1.00  ratio=2.31
eps=0.1: answer=  52.5  avg_err=10.00  ratio=1.10
e^1=2.72  e^0.1=1.11
```

- Reading the output
  - At ε = 1 the answer is off by about 1 on average; at ε = 0.1, by about 10
  - `ratio` compares how often the released answer exceeds 42.5 when the true count is 43 (one extra person) versus 42; it stays under e<sup>ε</sup>, which is the DP guarantee
  - The trade-off is utility against privacy; choose epsilon per use case and track the budget

### Accountability and awareness training
- ==Accountability==: a named person or body owns each AI system's outcomes and can pause or retire it
  - "The model decided" is never an acceptable answer to a regulator or a customer
  - Approval records, an audit trail of changes, and an incident process make accountability provable
  - Human oversight for high-impact decisions: a person can review, override, or stop the system
- ==Awareness training==: everyone who uses or builds AI understands its limits and the rules
  - All staff: hallucinations, what data may never go into a prompt, how to report a problem
  - Builders: secure design, bias testing, the approval process
  - Leaders: risk appetite and their accountability
  - The EU AI Act (Article 4) requires providers and deployers to take measures supporting AI literacy among their staff

:::exam
- Adding statistical noise so no single record can be inferred from a result is differential privacy; a smaller epsilon is more private and less accurate.
- "Users cannot understand why the model denied the request" is an explainability gap. "Users did not know an AI was involved" is a transparency gap.
- The same prompt giving different answers after a model update is a consistency problem; pin versions and run regression tests.
:::
