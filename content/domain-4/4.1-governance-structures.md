### Why AI needs its own governance
- ==AI governance== is the set of people, decisions, and rules that decide which AI the organization builds or buys, how it is used, and who answers for the result
  - Same goal as IT governance: business value at an acceptable risk
  - Different failure modes: a model can be wrong, biased, or leak data while every server is patched
- Questions governance must be able to answer on any given day
  - Which AI systems do we run, and who owns each one?
  - What data does each one touch?
  - Who approved it, against which policy, and when is it reviewed again?
- Without a structure, AI arrives anyway
  - Teams sign up for SaaS assistants, paste data into public chatbots, and wire models into workflows with no review
  - That is ==shadow AI== (objective 4.2); a working governance structure is the long-term fix
- Governance sits above controls
  - Controls (objective 2.x) are the gateway rules, access policies, and monitoring
  - Governance decides which controls are mandatory, for which systems, and who signs off
  - The organization's ==risk appetite== sets how far each control goes

### Organizational structures
- ==Organizational structures== for AI answer two questions: where the AI experts sit, and who has authority to approve or stop an AI use case
- Three common operating models

:::wide
| Model | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Centralized | One AI team builds and approves everything | Consistent standards, easy to audit | Bottleneck; far from business problems |
| ==Federated== (hub and spoke) | A central hub sets standards and shared platforms; AI staff embedded in business units build | Speed plus consistency; the usual target | Needs clear hub authority or spokes drift |
| Decentralized | Each business unit runs its own AI | Fast, close to the business | Duplicate tools, uneven security, shadow AI |
:::

- Committees that sit across the operating model
  - ==AI governance committee== (also called an AI steering committee or AI council): executives from security, legal, privacy, risk, data, HR, and the business; approves high-risk use cases and sets risk appetite
  - AI ethics or review board: examines fairness and societal impact of sensitive use cases (hiring, lending, health)
  - Board of directors: oversight of AI strategy and material AI risk; receives regular reporting
- Executive accountability
  - Many organizations name a Chief AI Officer or give the role to the CIO, CDO, or CISO
  - The point is one named executive who owns the AI risk register
- The ==three lines model== applies to AI as it does to any risk
  - First line: the teams that build and run AI systems own the risk day to day
  - Second line: risk, compliance, privacy, and security set policy and challenge the first line
  - Third line: internal audit gives independent assurance to the board

### AI Center of Excellence
- An ==AI Center of Excellence== (CoE) is the hub of a federated model: a small cross-functional team that makes AI repeatable and safe across the organization
- What a CoE typically owns
  - Reference architectures and approved platforms (model gateway, vector store, MLOps pipeline)
  - Reusable patterns: an approved RAG template, prompt libraries, evaluation harnesses
  - The ==AI inventory== (system register) and the use-case intake process
  - Training: role-based AI literacy, secure prompt and agent design
  - Vendor and model evaluations so each team does not repeat them
- Who staffs it
  - AI architects, ML and platform engineers, a governance engineer, and liaisons from security, legal, and privacy
- How it fails
  - Becomes an ivory tower that only writes documents; teams route around it
  - Becomes a delivery team that builds everything; turns into a centralized bottleneck
- A CoE advises and enables; the governance committee decides
  - Keep approval authority with the committee so the CoE is not marking its own homework

### AI policies and procedures
- ==AI policies and procedures== turn the committee's decisions into rules people can follow
- Use the normal document hierarchy

| Document | Says | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Policy | What and why; mandatory | "Only approved AI services may process company data" |
| Standard | Specific, measurable rules | "Customer data may go only to tenants with a no-training clause" |
| Procedure | Step-by-step how | "How to request a new AI use case" |
| Guideline | Recommended practice | "Writing system prompts for internal assistants" |

- Policies most organizations write first
  - ==AI acceptable use policy==: which tools staff may use, which data they may enter, and that a human reviews output before it is relied on
  - AI development and model risk policy: testing, documentation, approval gates, and monitoring for models the organization builds
  - AI procurement and third-party policy: what a vendor must prove before purchase (objective 4.3)
  - Data handling for AI: classification rules for prompts, training sets, and RAG sources
- Procedures that make the policies real
  - Use-case intake and risk tiering
  - Pre-deployment review: red-team results, bias evaluation, security sign-off
  - AI incident response: a jailbreak, a data leak through a chatbot, a harmful output, a model rollback
  - Periodic review and decommissioning: retire a model and its data on a schedule
- Keep policies tool-neutral
  - Name data classes and approval levels, not product names; products change monthly
- Map policies to an external framework so auditors can follow them
  - ISO/IEC 42001 (AI management system) and the NIST AI RMF Govern function are the common anchors

:::try
- Write a one-page AI acceptable use policy for a 50-person IT department
  - Three data classes (public, internal, restricted) and which AI tools may receive each
  - One rule for human review of output, one for reporting a suspected leak
- Hand it to a colleague and ask which rule they would break first; rewrite that rule
:::

:::exam
- "A central team sets standards and platforms while business units build their own AI" describes a federated (hub and spoke) model, usually run by an AI Center of Excellence.
- A policy states what is required; a procedure gives the steps. A question asking for "step-by-step instructions to request a new AI tool" wants a procedure.
- Approval of high-risk AI use cases belongs to a cross-functional governance committee; a distractor will hand it to the data science team that built the system.
:::
